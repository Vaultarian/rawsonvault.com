P2 Electricity

How do charge, current, potential difference, and resistance relate — and what determines how devices behave in circuits?

Current is the rate of flow of charge: Q = It. Potential difference is the energy transferred per unit charge that moves between two points. Resistance opposes current flow — Ohm's Law (V = IR) holds for ohmic conductors at constant temperature, but not for diodes, filament bulbs, or thermistors. The I–V characteristic (graph of current against voltage) is the standard way to identify component behaviour: a straight line through the origin indicates an ohmic conductor; a curve indicates non-ohmic behaviour.

In series circuits, current is the same everywhere; potential differences add up. In parallel circuits, current splits; the potential difference is the same across each branch. These rules come directly from conservation of energy and conservation of charge. Power dissipated in a component = VI = I²R, which is why the National Grid transmits at very high voltage and very low current — reducing I dramatically cuts I²R heating losses in cables.

A 12 Ω resistor and a 6 Ω resistor are connected in series to a 9 V supply. (a) Calculate the current in the circuit. (b) Calculate the potential difference across each resistor. (c) Calculate the power dissipated in the 12 Ω resistor. What would the combined resistance be if they were connected in parallel instead?

What students must understand

  • Standard circuit symbols; drawing and interpreting circuit diagrams
  • Charge, current, and time: Q = It; current measured in amperes
  • Conventional current direction (positive to negative); electron flow opposite
  • Potential difference as energy transferred per unit charge; measured in volts
  • Resistance: V = IR (Ohm's Law); ohmic conductors at constant temperature
  • I–V graphs: linear (ohmic resistor), filament bulb (increasing resistance), diode (one-way)
  • Series circuits: same current; p.d.s add; total resistance = sum of individual resistances
  • Parallel circuits: same p.d.; currents add; total resistance less than smallest branch
  • Thermistors: resistance decreases as temperature increases (used in thermostats)
  • LDRs: resistance decreases as light intensity increases (used in light-dependent switches)
  • Mains electricity: 230 V AC, 50 Hz; live, neutral, earth; three-pin plugs and fuses
  • Power dissipated: P = VI = I²R; energy E = Pt = QV
  • National Grid: step-up transformers for transmission (high V, low I) → less I²R loss
  • HT Transformer equation: Vp/Vs = Np/Ns; VpIp = VsIs
