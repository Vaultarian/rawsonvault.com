Cooling Air and Argon

From 20 °C down to −190 °C: which substances are solid, liquid or gas, and what the particles are doing.

A sample of air is sealed inside a container at 20 °C. The container is cooled steadily until the air inside reaches −190 °C.

(a) Air is a mixture. Three of the substances in it are oxygen, nitrogen and carbon dioxide. Use the melting and boiling points below to work out whether each one is a solid, a liquid or a gas at −190 °C.

(b) The air also holds a little argon. On the way down from 20 °C to −190 °C the argon condenses and then freezes. Describe how the arrangement and the movement of the argon particles change as it cools, and say what happens to the energy of the particles.

Reading a state from two temperatures

The rule

  • Colder than the melting point: solid.
  • Between the melting point and the boiling point: liquid.
  • Warmer than the boiling point: gas.
  • With negative temperatures, the bigger number after the minus sign is the colder one: −190 °C is colder than −183 °C.

The three states of argon

  • Gas: far apart, random arrangement, moving quickly in every direction.
  • Liquid: touching, random arrangement, sliding past each other.
  • Solid: touching, regular pattern, vibrating about fixed positions.

A model answer, one step at a time

  1. (a) Oxygen−190 °C lies between oxygen's melting point (−219 °C) and its boiling point (−183 °C), so the oxygen is a liquid.
  2. (a) Nitrogen−190 °C is warmer than nitrogen's boiling point (−196 °C), so the nitrogen is still a gas.
  3. (a) Carbon dioxide−190 °C is colder than −78 °C, the temperature at which carbon dioxide sublimates, so the carbon dioxide is a solid.
  4. (b) Argon as a gas, cooling from 20 °CThe particles are far apart in a random arrangement and move quickly in all directions. As the temperature falls they move more slowly, because their kinetic energy decreases.
  5. (b) Condensing at −186 °CThe argon changes from a gas to a liquid. The particles come together until they are touching. The arrangement is still random, and the particles now slide past each other. Bonds form between the particles, so their potential energy decreases while the temperature stays the same.
  6. (b) Freezing at −189 °CThe argon changes from a liquid to a solid. The particles settle into a regular pattern and each one vibrates about a fixed position. More bonds form, so the potential energy decreases again.
  7. (b) The energy overallFrom 20 °C to −190 °C the internal energy of the argon decreases the whole way: through Ek while the temperature is falling, and through Ep at each change of state.
Show a full answer

(a) At −190 °C oxygen is a liquid, nitrogen is a gas and carbon dioxide is a solid.

(b) At 20 °C argon is a gas: its particles are far apart, randomly arranged and moving quickly in all directions. As the air cools, the particles slow down because their kinetic energy decreases. At −186 °C the argon condenses. The particles are now touching, still randomly arranged, and they slide past each other. At −189 °C the argon freezes. The particles are held in a regular pattern and vibrate about fixed positions. At each change of state bonds form between the particles, so their potential energy decreases. The internal energy of the argon decreases throughout.
