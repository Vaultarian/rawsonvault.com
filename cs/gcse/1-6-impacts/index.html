§1.6 Ethical, Legal, Cultural and Environmental Impacts

How has digital technology changed society — and what laws govern its use?

Technology does not exist in a vacuum. Every digital system has consequences — for individuals, organisations, and the planet — and those consequences spread across five categories: ethical (what is right), legal (what is permitted), cultural (how societies and behaviours change), environmental (the physical cost of computing), and privacy (how personal data is collected and used). The OCR exam's 8-mark extended-response question most often lands here — and it rewards balanced argument, not one-sided lists.

UK law sets hard limits. The Data Protection Act 2018 governs how personal data must be collected, stored, and used. The Computer Misuse Act 1990 criminalises unauthorised access to computer systems. The Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988 protects creators from having their work copied without permission. Software licences add a further layer: open-source software makes the source code freely available; proprietary software keeps it locked. Each model has genuine advantages and disadvantages, and the exam asks students to recommend one for a described scenario.

8-mark question style: "Evaluate the impact of social media on society." — You must: identify impacts across multiple categories; give benefits AND drawbacks; build a sustained argument; reach a balanced conclusion. Draft a plan: 4 points × 2 marks each (point + development).

The three Acts — one line each

Data Protection Act 2018: organisations must collect only necessary personal data, keep it secure, and not share it without consent
Computer Misuse Act 1990: criminalises unauthorised access to computer systems, unauthorised modification of data, and distributing malware
Copyright Designs and Patents Act 1988: protects creators' work — software, music, text, images — from being copied or distributed without permission

What students must understand

  • Five impact categories: ethical, legal, cultural, environmental, privacy — with examples for each
  • Ethical: algorithmic bias, AI decision-making, surveillance, digital divide
  • Legal: copyright infringement, hacking, data misuse — and the three named Acts
  • Cultural: social media changing communication, misinformation, remote work, screen time
  • Environmental: energy consumption of data centres, e-waste, carbon footprint of devices
  • Privacy: data collection by apps and platforms, tracking, surveillance cameras, GDPR
  • DPA 2018: 8 principles for handling personal data; organisations must have a lawful basis for processing
  • Computer Misuse Act 1990: three levels of offence — unauthorised access, unauthorised access with intent to commit further crimes, unauthorised modification
  • Copyright Act 1988: protects software, music, images, writing; violation = infringement; open-source is an alternative legal model
  • Open-source licence: source code is publicly available; free to use, modify, and share; anyone can contribute
  • Proprietary licence: source code is closed; users pay to use the software; company controls updates and features
  • Recommend a licence type for a scenario: open-source suits community projects, schools, tight budgets; proprietary suits commercial software needing IP protection and dedicated support
