Oscillations & Waves

Movement can cause useful and problematic consequences in physical systems

This Year 9 unit studies regular, repeating motion — oscillations — and the waves that carry it. Students measure frequency and period, investigate the swing of a pendulum, and explore how vibrations travel through different substances. The conceptual heart of the unit is resonance: how a system driven at its natural frequency can build up large oscillations, and how that can be both useful and destructive, from musical instruments to bridges that have to be engineered against it.

The key concept is systems, with related concepts of movement and consequences, in the global context of scientific and technical innovation. The unit's debatable question — whether the cost of building oscillation-proof structures is always worth it — gives students a genuine engineering trade-off to reason about.

Statement of inquiry: Movement can cause useful and problematic consequences in physical systems.

Inquiry questions include: What are the effects of oscillations on objects? How can we build systems to mitigate the consequences of oscillations? Is the cost of building oscillation-proof structures always worth it?

What students learn

  • Calculating frequency and period, and the relationship between them
  • The frequency and period of a pendulum, and what changes them
  • How waves carry energy and how their speed differs between substances
  • Resonance — what it is, where it occurs, and why it matters for real structures
  • How engineers design systems to manage or suppress unwanted oscillations

Activities & investigations

  • Frequency and period calculation work, and a pendulum investigation
  • Researching and presenting the speed of sound in different substances
  • A research project into resonance and how engineers solve the problems it causes

How it is assessed

The unit is assessed with a strong criterion D focus. Students complete a research project describing where resonance occurs and how engineers address it, discussing the implications of the science, applying scientific language effectively, and documenting their sources — the four strands of reflecting on the impacts of science.
