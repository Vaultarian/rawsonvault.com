A

The pendulum

A pendulum is a mass on a string that swings back and forth. One complete oscillation is out and back to where you started.

The period T is the time for one complete oscillation, in seconds. The frequency f is the number of oscillations per second, in hertz, and f = 1/T.

How to read the set-up

  • Measure the length L from the pivot to the centre of the bob.
  • The release angle θ is the angle between the string and the resting position when you let go.
  • The release height h is how far above its resting point you lift the bob.
  • Time how long the bob takes to swing out and back, and call that the period T.

B.iii

How you will measure the period: time 20 swings

Thirteen students time the same pendulum. Its true period T is 1.00 s.

Press button 1 to collect the one-swing times.

Starting and stopping a stopwatch by hand adds a reaction time of about 0.2 s at each end. On a swing of one second, that is a big share of the answer.

Time 20 swings and divide by 20. The same reaction error is spread across twenty swings, so it moves the period by a twentieth as much.

Method sentence to copy: I will time 20 swings on a stopwatch with a resolution of 0.01 s, divide by 20 to find the period T, and repeat three times at each length.

Count “zero” as you start the watch, and count one each time the bob returns to where it started.

C.i

Your results: the expected data, one length at a time

Five elements of a data table

  • Headings with the unit written once, in the heading.
  • Three trials, then a mean.
  • Same decimal places all the way down a column.
  • Independent variable on the left, dependent variable on the right.
  • Ruled lines round every cell.

The first check to make

Your 25 cm pendulum should land near T = 1.0 s. A table that gives about 0.5 s counted half swings as whole ones, and one near 1.05 s counted from one instead of zero.

C.i

Your graph: a line of best fit, not join the dots

Variable:

Every timing carries a small error, so real points sit a little above and below the true pattern. A graph should show the pattern.

Six things the marker looks for

  • A title: the period against the variable you changed.
  • Both axes labelled, with units.
  • The independent variable along the bottom, the period T up the side.
  • A scale that fills the paper, in even steps.
  • Small crosses, one per reading.
  • One line of best fit, drawn through the middle of the trend.

Both variables are numbers, so this is a scatter graph. A flat line of best fit is a finding.

C.iv

Was your method valid?

A method is valid if it gave you results you can trust. Change how the experiment is run and watch the checklist respond.

    Write about the design and the data: the spread of the points, the repeats, the instruments, the controls.

    What this question is really asking

    Validity is a question about your method, and your data is the evidence. Ask yourself: is there a fundamental problem with the way I designed and ran this experiment? A clear pattern says the method is working. Random or messy results say something in the method is wrong. Getting the answer you expected is a separate matter.

    1 · Look at your data
    The three trials, the graph, the points, the line of best fit.
    2 · Ask what its pattern says
    Tidy and repeatable points to a sound method. Scattered or random points to a problem with the design.
    3 · Decide, and say because
    Valid, partly valid or not valid, with the evidence from steps 1 and 2.

    The rule on this page: trials are close together when the range (biggest minus smallest) is 2% of the mean or less. For a period near 1 s that is 0.02 s, and the stopwatch reads to 0.01 s.
