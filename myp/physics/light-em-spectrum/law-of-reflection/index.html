The Law of Reflection

Year 8 Physics · Unit 1: Light & EM Spectrum · Mr. Rawson. Work down the packet in order, or use the list on the left to jump to any part.

1

Two kinds of reflection

Light bounces off every surface it lands on. What changes from one surface to the next is which way the reflected rays go.

A smooth surface: specular reflection. A mirror is flat on the scale that matters to light. Rays that arrive parallel leave parallel, still in formation. The pattern survives the bounce, so your eye can rebuild it and you see an image.
A rough surface: diffuse reflection. Paper feels smooth but close up it is a landscape of bumps, each one facing a slightly different way. Rays that arrive parallel leave in all directions. The pattern is destroyed, so there is no image, but light still reaches your eye from every part of the page, which is why you can see the paper itself.
Every scattered ray still obeys the law. Each tiny bump has its own normal (the dashed lines) pointing its own way, so the same law gives a different answer at every point on the surface.
Check: choose any ray above. Its i and r match.

3

One ray, and why we can draw it

The bulb sends light out in every direction. The single slit on the ray box blocks nearly all of it and lets one narrow strip through, so you get one clean straight line you can trace.

We draw that line with an arrow on it and call it a ray: a straight line showing the direction light travels. It is a model, not an object you could pick up.

Check: make the slit narrower. The strip gets thinner and its middle is easier to find, so the line you trace is sharper.
4

Using a protractor

A protractor has two scales, counting opposite ways. Only one is right.

  1. Centre mark on your dot.
  2. Baseline along the normal.
  3. Read the scale that starts at 0 on your line.
If it looks wrong, it probably is. Reading the wrong scale gives 180° minus the true angle (20° reads 160°). Measuring from the mirror gives 90° minus the true angle (20° reads 70°).
Check: set the true angle to 20° and try both baselines. Only one combination gives a reading of 20°. The other readings tell you what went wrong.

5

Measure the law

Kit: ray box with a single slit, plane mirror, protractor, ruler, sharp pencil, plain paper.

Care: the lamp gets hot, so carry the box by the case. Never shine it at anyone's eyes.
6

Your five trials

Do each trial in its own box: mirror line, dot, dashed normal, then trace both rays through the dot. Write your two angles underneath.

Expected. In every trial r matches i to within a degree or two. A pair that adds up to 90° (for example 20° and 70°) means the angle was measured from the mirror, so measure again from the normal. A reading over 90° means the other protractor scale was read.

8

Extension: predict, then test

Predict, then test. If the angle of incidence goes up by 10°, what happens to the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray? And if you keep the ray still but tilt the mirror by 10°, what does the reflected ray do? Write your prediction, then try both with your kit.
A. The angle between the rays is i + r = 2i, so it grows by 20°. Press the button and watch the number.
B. Tilting the mirror by 10° swings the reflected ray by 20°. The normal tilts with the mirror, which changes both i and r.

Both results come from the same law. The ray bends away from the normal by the same amount on each side, so any change to the mirror counts twice.
